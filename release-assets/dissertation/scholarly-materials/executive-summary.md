## Overview

Jay Rosen's 1986 doctoral dissertation challenges the prevailing assumption that providing more and better information is the primary function of the press in a democracy. Written under the supervision of Neil Postman, author of *Amusing Ourselves to Death*, the dissertation argues that "providing information is not all there is to informing the public"—and that the conditions which enable a modern press to grow and thrive are often the same conditions that undermine informed public discourse.

The dissertation traces how the press, from its origins as an extension of face-to-face political speech, evolved into a commercial enterprise that constitutes its own kind of "public realm"—one governed by the competition for attention rather than the requirements of democratic deliberation. In this new environment, the very concept of "the public" has been transformed, and the press has become, in Rosen's formulation, "impossible": structurally incapable of fulfilling the democratic functions assigned to it.

---

## Central Thesis

The relationship between press and public cannot be understood simply by examining the accuracy, fairness, and completeness of news coverage. Instead, it must be viewed "ecologically"—set within a particular social context where the world is "symbolized and summed-up, arranged and distorted by a number of communication media." The dissertation's central finding is that **the professional attitude toward journalism—focused exclusively on information delivery—is a deeply limited view that ignores fundamental transformations in the nature of public life itself.**

---

## Key Arguments

### 1. The Ecological View of Press and Public

Rosen argues that journalism must be understood "not as something journalists do, but as a transaction between producers and consumers, writers and readers, the press and the public." This transaction takes place within a symbolic environment shaped by multiple media, economic pressures, and social conditions. Focusing solely on whether the news is accurate, fair, and complete misses the larger picture.

### 2. Five Factors Beyond Information

The dissertation identifies five factors that shape the relationship between press and public beyond the content of the news itself:

1. **The size and scale of the world to be informed about** — As communication improves, so does the complexity of the world that must be communicated about. The same technologies that bring news from distant places also expand the realm of public problems.

2. **The character of the social bond among the audience** — The less substantial the common activity members of an audience share, the more journalism tends toward producing attention rather than information. "Sensationalism is what happens when communication outruns the sense of community."

3. **Structural characteristics of mass media** — Mass communication privatizes reception (news arrives in the home, to isolated individuals) and sponsors a permanent competition for attention that shapes all public discourse.

4. **The nature of language and symbols** — News, like any symbol system, is necessarily incomplete and shaped by conventions. "Presenting 'all the news' is an impossible goal, and the press that avows it can only be an impossible press."

5. **The professional attitude** — The belief that accurate, fair, and complete reporting will automatically produce an informed public obscures all the other factors that determine whether information actually functions as information.

### 3. Attention Granted vs. Attention Gained

In traditional public settings like the town meeting, attention was *granted* by citizens who gathered to discuss common concerns. In the new public realm constituted by mass media, attention must be *gained* against competitors. This shift transforms public discourse: "everything said is devoted to the struggle to be heard." The content of public life is eclipsed by the competition for attention.

### 4. The Impossible Press

The chapter that gives the dissertation its title examines how journalism became a "profession" in response to the conflict between the newspaper's commercial basis and its public functions. Professionalism promised that trained journalists of good character could fulfill the democratic duties of the press even within a business dedicated to profit. But this framed a structural conflict as a matter of personal conduct, obscuring the fundamental tension between commercial imperatives and public service.

---

## Historical Significance

The dissertation was written before the internet, before social media, before the 24-hour news cycle, and before the current crisis of trust in journalism. Yet its analysis anticipates many of the problems that would unfold over the following four decades:

- **The attention economy**: Rosen's analysis of how commercial pressures transform journalism into an "attention-producing machine" presages contemporary debates about clickbait, engagement metrics, and algorithmic amplification.

- **The decline of shared public life**: The dissertation's concern with the privatization of news consumption and the fragmentation of common understanding anticipates today's "filter bubbles" and polarized media environments.

- **The limits of "objectivity"**: The critique of the professional attitude—the belief that accurate reporting alone serves democracy—prefigures ongoing debates about whether traditional objectivity norms are adequate to contemporary challenges.

- **The structural vs. individual framing**: Rosen's insistence that journalism's problems are structural rather than a matter of individual bias or incompetence remains relevant to discussions about media reform.

---

## Relevance to Jay Rosen's Subsequent Work

The dissertation provides the intellectual foundation for concepts Jay Rosen would develop over the following decades:

- **"The View from Nowhere"** — The professional attitude described in the dissertation evolves into Rosen's critique of a journalistic stance that claims to stand outside politics and perspective.

- **"The People Formerly Known as the Audience"** — The dissertation's analysis of how audiences are "assembled" by media, rather than pre-existing as communities, anticipates Rosen's later work on how digital technology transforms the audience from passive consumers into active participants.

- **Public Journalism** — The dissertation's Deweyan concern with whether "the public" can function as an active democratic agent leads directly to Rosen's involvement in the public journalism movement of the 1990s.

- **PressThink** — The blog Rosen founded in 2003 continues the dissertation's project of examining the press "ecologically," as an institution embedded in broader social and political contexts.

---

## Concluding Insight

The dissertation ends with a warning that remains urgent nearly four decades later:

> "For any press anywhere, making things public does not a public make."

The existence of a free press does not automatically produce an informed public capable of democratic self-governance. The conditions that would allow information to function as information—a world scaled to human understanding, communities that share common concerns, media structures that support rather than undermine deliberation—must themselves be created and maintained. Without attention to these conditions, even the most accurate and complete journalism will fail to serve democracy.
