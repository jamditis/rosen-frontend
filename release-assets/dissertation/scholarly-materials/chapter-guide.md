# Chapter-by-Chapter Guide: "The Impossible Press"

**Status:** COMPLETE

---

## Structure Overview

The dissertation is organized into two parts plus introduction and conclusion:

- **Introduction:** Journalism as a Transaction
- **Part One:** The Making of the Modern Public (Chapters 1-5)
- **Part Two:** The Public and the Professionalized Press (Chapters 6-8)
- **Conclusion:** Toward an Ecological View of Press and Public

---

## Introduction: Journalism as a Transaction

### Synopsis

The introduction establishes the dissertation's central argument: that journalism must be understood not as something journalists do, but as a transaction between producers and consumers, writers and readers, the press and the public. This transaction takes place within a specific social and symbolic environment that shapes what information can mean.

Rosen challenges the conventional view that the press simply "informs the public," arguing that this formulation assumes too much about both the press and the public. The introduction sets up an "ecological" approach that examines the relationship between press and public within a broader context of social organization, communication media, and symbolic environments.

### Key Concepts Introduced
- Journalism as transaction
- The ecological view of press and public
- The gap between "providing information" and "informing the public"
- The symbolic environment

### Notable Quotes
> "Journalism needs to be thought of, not as something journalists do, but as a transaction between producers and consumers, writers and readers, the press and the public."

### Reading Notes
The introduction establishes the theoretical framework that will guide the entire dissertation. Pay attention to how Rosen distinguishes his approach from conventional press criticism focused on accuracy and bias.

## Part One: The Making of the Modern Public

### Chapter 1: Democracy and Distance

#### Synopsis

Chapter 1 examines how news arises as a social necessity when the scale of events extends beyond word of mouth. Rosen argues that "news arrives from a distance"—it crosses the space between individuals and events. The chapter traces how the American republic, by extending democracy over distance, created the need for an enterprising press.

The chapter contrasts small-scale democracy (where citizens can see with their eyes and hear with their ears most everything they need to know) with large-scale republican government (which requires newspapers to keep citizens informed of distant events). Before the penny press of the 1830s, newspapers provided almost no local news because communities were small enough that local events spread naturally through word of mouth.

#### Key Concepts
- News as a function of distance
- The "imagined community" of newspaper readers
- The distinction between village democracy and republican scale
- How the press helps form publics, not just inform them

#### Notable Quotes
> "News arrives from a distance. It crosses the space between individuals and events."

> "The sort of public for which a press is required begins to be formed when the interests of a community are extended beyond the scale of face to face relations."

#### Reading Notes
This chapter draws heavily on Benedict Anderson's concept of "imagined communities" and sets up the tension between scale and democratic competence that runs throughout the dissertation.

---

### Chapter 2: Two Views of News

#### Synopsis

Chapter 2 examines the penny press revolution of the 1830s, which transformed the newspaper from a special interest journal serving merchants and political elites into a mass medium reaching ordinary citizens. The chapter distinguishes between two functions of news: information (which requires action based on fresh data) and human interest (which orients and entertains).

Rosen argues that the city created the need for a new form of news—the human interest story—which helped urbanites make sense of their fragmented environment. The chapter traces how the penny papers discovered that people wanted to learn about events in their own environment and developed the techniques of popular journalism.

#### Key Concepts
- Information vs. human interest functions of news
- The penny press revolution (1830s)
- The city as a "breaking apart of the common world"
- Street sales as feedback mechanism

#### Notable Quotes
> "Urbanization represents a kind of breaking apart of the common space in which people once dwelled, a division of the community into many separate worlds."

> "The contacts of the city may indeed be face to face, but they are nevertheless impersonal, superficial, transitory and segmental." (quoting Louis Wirth)

#### Reading Notes
The distinction between news as information and news as human interest is crucial to understanding Rosen's later critique of the "professional attitude." The city becomes a key concept—not just a location but a type of social environment.

---

### Chapter 3: The Universal Town Meeting

#### Synopsis

Chapter 3 critiques the vision of the press as a "universal town meeting"—the idea that the newspaper extends the scale of democratic participation while preserving its essential character. Rosen argues that this vision ignores fundamental contradictions between the conditions that enable a modern press and those that sustain genuine public deliberation.

The chapter examines the Great Moon Hoax of 1835, in which the New York Sun reported the discovery of life on the moon, as an example of how the penny papers appealed to audiences by completing images already dimly drawn in readers' minds rather than simply conveying facts. Accuracy becomes "relative"—a function of fit with audience expectations rather than correspondence with external reality.

#### Key Concepts
- The "universal town meeting" myth
- Accuracy as fit with audience expectations
- Street sales as feedback
- The circulation of information vs. the formation of opinion

#### Notable Quotes
> "Accuracy, then, is a relative term. When news functions as information, something resembling scientific standards can prevail. But when the functions of news are symbolic, accuracy is a statement about the audience and what it needs or desires."

#### Reading Notes
This chapter anticipates contemporary debates about "engagement" metrics and the tension between what audiences want to read and what they need to know.

---

### Chapter 4: From Crowd to Public

#### Synopsis

Chapter 4 examines turn-of-the-century crowd psychology and its relevance to understanding the newspaper audience. Rosen draws on Gustave LeBon, Gabriel Tarde, and other theorists who attempted to understand how people could act collectively without physically gathering together.

The key insight is that the newspaper creates a new type of social body—one that is neither a traditional community nor a physical crowd, but something in between. The "public" assembled by the press shares collective attention without sharing physical space, common activity, or pre-existing ties.

#### Key Concepts
- The crowd vs. the public
- Collective attention without physical contact
- Hypnosis and "suggestion" as metaphors for media influence
- The newspaper as a new form of social bond

#### Notable Quotes
> "A crowd, in the sense I am employing the word, can be formed in a hundred other ways than by mere physical presence together at one time and place. Printing, the telegraph, and the various modern inventions have made crowd-formation possible without physical contact."

#### Reading Notes
This chapter shows Rosen engaging with classic social theory (LeBon, Tarde, Freud) to understand a genuinely new phenomenon—mass audiences assembled by media.

---

### Chapter 5: Communication Without Community

#### Synopsis

Chapter 5 explores what it means for people to be connected by media without forming genuine communities. Rosen argues that the mass circulation newspaper represents "communication without community"—a form of connection that lacks the shared activity, mutual recognition, and common purpose of traditional community life.

The chapter examines how entertainment functions in a mass society: as "the search for those symbolic forms that will bring members of an audience together on no other, more solid basis than the common consumption of the forms themselves." Johnny Carson is analyzed as an exemplar of this empty form of communication.

#### Key Concepts
- Communication without community
- The "space of collected attention"
- Entertainment as audience assembly
- The mass as a new form of social body
- Public transmission, private reception

#### Notable Quotes
> "Sensationalism is what happens when communication outruns the sense of community."

> "The readers of the paper were publicly addressed but privately situated."

#### Reading Notes
This chapter's analysis of "attention space" and the assembly of audiences for commercial purposes anticipates contemporary attention economy theory by several decades.

## Part Two: The Public and the Professionalized Press

### Chapter 6: The Impossible Press

#### Synopsis

The title chapter examines the professionalization of journalism around the turn of the twentieth century. Rosen argues that professionalism emerged as a response to the conflict between the newspaper's commercial basis and its public functions—but that it framed a structural problem as a matter of individual conduct.

The chapter traces the transformation of the newspaper from a craft controlled by independent printers to a capital-intensive business requiring massive investment. It examines the "golden age" of press criticism (1890s-1930s) when observers recognized that journalism had become primarily a profit-seeking business, and explores how professionalism promised to reconcile commercial pressures with public service.

#### Key Concepts
- The impossible press: structural conflict between commerce and public service
- Attention granted vs. attention gained
- The "golden age" of press criticism
- Professionalism as ideology
- The "whig interpretation" of journalism history

#### Notable Quotes
> "The newspaper had become an attention producing machine which really had no other goal."

> "By setting up a permanent and escalating competition for attention, mass communication institutionalizes the insecurity of the public realm."

> "In a traditional public realm—the Greek polis, the town meeting—attention is granted, while in the new public realm created by the mass media attention has to be gained."

#### Reading Notes
This is the dissertation's most important chapter. The distinction between "attention granted" and "attention gained" is a key theoretical contribution.

---

### Chapter 7: The Myth of the Omnicompetent Citizen

#### Synopsis

Chapter 7 examines the democratic ideal of the "omnicompetent citizen"—the notion that citizens can and should be informed about all matters of public concern. Drawing on Walter Lippmann's critique in *Public Opinion*, Rosen argues that this ideal is impossible to realize and that its persistence distorts our understanding of what journalism can accomplish.

The chapter explores the Lippmann-Dewey debate about democracy and public opinion, showing how different conceptions of the citizen lead to different expectations for the press.

#### Key Concepts
- The omnicompetent citizen myth
- Lippmann's critique of democratic competence
- The gap between democratic ideals and democratic realities
- Stereotypes and the "pictures in our heads"

#### Notable Quotes
> The ideal of the "omnicompetent citizen" who can understand and judge all public affairs is a "false ideal" that sets democracy up for failure.

#### Reading Notes
This chapter engages directly with classic texts in democratic theory (Lippmann, Dewey) and shows how assumptions about citizens shape expectations for journalism.

---

### Chapter 8: The Art and Science of Forming a Public

#### Synopsis

Chapter 8 examines the question of whether and how a genuine public can be formed under modern conditions. Drawing on John Dewey's response to Lippmann, Rosen explores the possibility of rebuilding public life without retreating to a mythical village past.

The chapter examines Thomas Jefferson's ward system as an alternative vision of democratic organization—one that maintains face-to-face deliberation while scaling up to national scope.

#### Key Concepts
- Forming a public vs. informing a public
- Dewey's democratic faith
- Jefferson's ward system
- The conditions for democratic competence
- Reconstruction of community

#### Notable Quotes
> "Citizens do not form a public until they collectively understand the problems which concern them as citizens."

#### Reading Notes
This chapter points toward the public journalism movement that Rosen would become deeply involved with in the 1990s.

## Conclusion: Toward an Ecological View of Press and Public

### Synopsis

The conclusion synthesizes the dissertation's arguments into an "ecological" view of press and public. Rosen identifies five factors that shape the relationship between press and public beyond the content of news: the size of the world to be informed about, the character of social bonds among the audience, structural characteristics of mass media, the nature of language and symbols, and the professional attitude.

The conclusion argues that "the professional attitude is a very limited view of press and public" because it focuses exclusively on the accuracy and completeness of information while ignoring the conditions that determine whether information can function as information.

### Key Takeaways

1. "Providing information is not all there is to informing the public."
2. The conditions enabling a modern press are often the same conditions undermining public life.
3. Five factors beyond information content shape the press-public relationship.
4. The professional attitude obscures structural problems by framing them as matters of individual conduct.
5. "For any press anywhere, making things public does not a public make."

### Connection to Later Work

The conclusion points directly toward concepts Rosen would develop over the following decades:
- The "View from Nowhere" extends the critique of the professional attitude
- "The People Formerly Known as the Audience" builds on the analysis of audience passivity
- Public journalism applies Dewey's democratic theory to journalistic practice
- PressThink continues the ecological approach to press criticism
