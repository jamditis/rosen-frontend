# Syllabus-Ready Materials: "The Impossible Press"

**Status:** COMPLETE

---

## For Instructors

This document provides ready-to-use materials for incorporating "The Impossible Press" into journalism, media studies, and communication courses. All materials can be adapted to different course levels and contexts.

---

## Suggested Course Contexts

### Journalism History
- **Context:** Evolution of American journalism criticism
- **Pairing:** Walter Lippmann's *Public Opinion*, John Dewey's *The Public and Its Problems*
- **Focus:** How Rosen synthesizes and extends the Lippmann-Dewey debate

### Media Theory
- **Context:** Critical approaches to journalism as institution
- **Pairing:** Neil Postman's *Amusing Ourselves to Death*, Marshall McLuhan's *Understanding Media*
- **Focus:** The ecological/environmental approach to media analysis (Rosen studied under Postman)

### Press and Democracy
- **Context:** Relationship between journalism and democratic participation
- **Pairing:** Contemporary readings on media trust crisis, platform governance
- **Focus:** The structural constraints on journalism's democratic function

### Professional Ethics in Journalism
- **Context:** Professional identity and public responsibility
- **Pairing:** SPJ Code of Ethics, contemporary case studies on objectivity debates
- **Focus:** Rosen's critique of the "professional attitude" and its limits

### Communication and Community
- **Context:** Media's role in forming (or fragmenting) public life
- **Pairing:** Robert Putnam's *Bowling Alone*, danah boyd's *It's Complicated*
- **Focus:** "Communication without community" and its contemporary relevance

## Reading Assignments

### Single-Session Assignment (1-2 hours reading)

**Assign:** Introduction + Chapter 6 ("The Impossible Press") + Conclusion

This selection provides the dissertation's core argument in compressed form.

**Focus Questions:**
1. What does Rosen mean by "the impossible press"? What makes journalism structurally incapable of fulfilling its democratic functions?
2. What is the difference between "attention granted" and "attention gained"? Why does this distinction matter?
3. How does the "professional attitude" frame structural problems as matters of individual conduct?
4. What are the five factors that shape the press-public relationship beyond news content?

**Pre-Class Preparation:**
Students should come prepared to identify one contemporary example that illustrates Rosen's argument about the tension between commercial imperatives and public service.

### Two-Session Module

**Session 1:** Introduction + Part One (Chapters 1-5)
- **Reading time:** 3-4 hours
- **Focus:** Historical development of press-public relationship
- **Key concepts:** News and distance, two views of news, crowd vs. public, communication without community

**Session 1 Discussion Prompts:**
1. How did the scale of American democracy create the need for newspapers?
2. What are the "two views of news" and how do they create different expectations for journalism?
3. What is "communication without community" and why is it a problem for democracy?

**Session 2:** Part Two (Chapters 6-8) + Conclusion
- **Reading time:** 3-4 hours
- **Focus:** Professional journalism and its limitations
- **Key concepts:** The impossible press, attention granted vs. gained, omnicompetent citizen myth, forming vs. informing

**Session 2 Discussion Prompts:**
1. What makes the press "impossible"? Is this inevtiable or could it be changed?
2. What is the "myth of the omnicompetent citizen" and why does it persist?
3. What is the difference between "forming a public" and "informing a public"?

## Discussion Questions by Section

### For Introduction
1. How does framing journalism as a "transaction" change how we evaluate news coverage?
2. What does it mean to view press and public "ecologically"?
3. Why is "providing information" not the same as "informing the public"?

### For Part One (Chapters 1-5)
1. How did the growth of cities transform the function of news?
2. What is the difference between the "crowd" assembled by newspapers and a traditional "public"?
3. Why does Rosen argue that "sensationalism is what happens when communication outruns the sense of community"?
4. How does the newspaper create "communication without community"?

### For Part Two (Chapters 6-8)
1. What structural conflict does professionalism attempt to resolve? Why does Rosen say it fails?
2. What is the "myth of the omnicompetent citizen" and why is it harmful?
3. How does John Dewey's response to Lippmann point toward a different role for journalism?
4. What would it mean to "form" a public rather than merely "inform" one?

### For Conclusion
1. What are the five factors beyond information that shape the press-public relationship?
2. Why is the "professional attitude" a limited view of journalism's problems?
3. How does the dissertation's argument connect to Rosen's later work (PressThink, public journalism, "view from nowhere")?

### Connecting to Today
1. How do Rosen's 1986 critiques apply to platform-era journalism?
2. Is the competition for attention more or less intense than it was in 1986?
3. Has digital media made the press more or less "impossible"?
4. Are there examples of digital media successfully "forming publics"?

## Essay Prompts

### Short Response (500-750 words)

1. **The Impossible Press Today**
   Apply Rosen's concept of "the impossible press" to a contemporary journalism controversy. How does his 1986 framework illuminate current challenges?

   *Suggested topics: algorithmic news feeds, subscription vs. advertising models, engagement metrics, AI-generated content*

2. **Attention Granted vs. Attention Gained**
   Analyze a specific news organization or platform using Rosen's distinction between "attention granted" and "attention gained." How does the need to gain attention shape its journalism?

3. **Communication Without Community**
   Does social media represent "communication without community" in Rosen's terms? Use specific examples to argue your position.

### Research Paper (2000-3000 words)

1. **From Dissertation to Digital**
   Trace how one concept from Rosen's 1986 dissertation evolved in his subsequent work. Use the Jay Rosen Digital Archive to identify key developments.

   *Suggested concepts: "the view from nowhere," "the people formerly known as the audience," public journalism, church of the savvy*

2. **The Lippmann-Dewey Debate Revisited**
   Rosen draws heavily on the debate between Walter Lippmann and John Dewey about democracy and public opinion. Research this debate and evaluate Rosen's use of it. Whose side does he take? Is his synthesis convincing?

3. **The Ecological View Applied**
   Apply Rosen's "ecological view" to analyze a contemporary media environment (local news, podcasting, Substack, TikTok news, etc.). What do the five factors reveal that content analysis alone would miss?

### Comparative Analysis

1. **Two Critics Compared**
   Compare Rosen's critique of professional journalism with one contemporary critic (Jeff Jarvis, Emily Bell, Margaret Sullivan, etc.). What has changed? What persists?

2. **1986 vs. 2025**
   Compare the conditions Rosen describes in 1986 with today's media environment. Which of his concerns have been validated? Which have been superseded? What new challenges would he need to address?

## Classroom Activities

### Activity 1: Concept Mapping (30-45 minutes)

**Materials:** Whiteboard or collaborative doc (Google Doc, Miro, etc.)

**Instructions:**
1. Divide students into small groups (3-4 people)
2. Assign each group a key concept from the dissertation: "the impossible press," "attention granted vs. gained," "communication without community," "the omnicompetent citizen," "ecological view"
3. Groups create a concept map showing:
   - Definition of the concept
   - How it connects to other dissertation concepts
   - One historical example from the dissertation
   - One contemporary example
4. Groups present their maps; class builds a master map showing connections

**Debrief Questions:**
- Which concepts are most central to Rosen's argument?
- Which concepts are most relevant to contemporary journalism?

---

### Activity 2: Then and Now (45-60 minutes)

**Materials:** Archive access, recent news examples, handout with 1986 vs. 2025 comparison categories

**Instructions:**
1. Present students with a specific critique from the dissertation (e.g., "everything said is devoted to the struggle to be heard")
2. Students find a contemporary example that either:
   - Confirms Rosen's critique still applies
   - Shows how conditions have changed
   - Demonstrates both continuity and change
3. Students present examples and discuss patterns
4. Class debate: Is the press more or less "impossible" today?

**Sample Comparison Categories:**
| 1986 | 2025 | Analysis |
|------|------|----------|
| Print advertising | Digital advertising / platforms | How has the attention economy changed? |
| Three TV networks | Infinite content options | Has fragmentation helped or hurt? |
| Newsroom gatekeepers | Algorithmic curation | Who controls the "attention space" now? |
| Mass audience | Niche audiences | Is this "communication without community" or many communities? |

---

### Activity 3: Archive Exploration (30-45 minutes)

**Materials:** Jay Rosen Digital Archive access, worksheets

**Instructions:**
1. Assign students (individually or in pairs) a concept from the dissertation
2. Students search the archive for later articles where Rosen develops that concept
3. Students answer:
   - How did Rosen first articulate this idea in 1986?
   - How did his thinking evolve over time?
   - What events or developments prompted that evolution?
4. Brief presentations: Each student/pair shares one key finding

**Suggested Concepts for Search:**
- "The view from nowhere"
- "The people formerly known as the audience"
- Public journalism / citizen journalism
- Objectivity and the professional attitude
- Attention and the press

---

### Activity 4: Stakeholder Debate (60-90 minutes)

**Setup:** Assign students roles representing different perspectives on journalism reform

**Roles:**
- Traditional newspaper editor defending professionalism
- Digital-native journalist arguing for engagement
- Media critic (channeling Rosen's arguments)
- Citizen/audience member
- Platform executive (Facebook, Google)
- Local news advocate

**Debate Questions:**
1. What is journalism for? What should its primary function be?
2. Is the "impossible press" a problem to be solved or a condition to be managed?
3. Who is responsible for "forming publics" in a democracy?

**Debrief:** How did Rosen's framework inform the debate? What solutions did it suggest or foreclose?

## Instructor Notes

### Common Student Questions

**Q: Why should we read a 1986 dissertation? Hasn't everything changed?**
A: The dissertation identifies structural tensions in journalism that persist despite technological change. The competition for attention, the conflict between commerce and public service, the gap between informing and forming publics—these problems have intensified, not disappeared. Students often find the 1986 perspective helps them see current problems more clearly.

**Q: Is Rosen too pessimistic about journalism?**
A: Rosen's critique is structural, not a condemnation of individual journalists. He's identifying constraints, not attributing blame. Discuss with students: Is identifying structural problems pessimistic, or is it a necessary first step toward addressing them?

**Q: What's the solution? What should journalism do?**
A: The dissertation is primarily diagnostic. Point students toward Rosen's later work on public journalism for constructive proposals. Note that his career has been dedicated to imagining alternatives, not just critiquing the status quo.

### Teaching Tips

- **Start with contemporary examples:** Before assigning the reading, discuss a current journalism controversy. Return to this example throughout the unit to show how Rosen's framework illuminates it.

- **Use the archive:** The Jay Rosen Digital Archive allows students to trace how dissertation ideas evolved. This demonstrates intellectual history in action.

- **Assign the introduction first:** The introduction is accessible and establishes the key framework. Students who understand the "ecological view" will better grasp the historical chapters.

- **Don't skip Chapter 6:** Even in abbreviated assignments, Chapter 6 ("The Impossible Press") is essential. It contains the dissertation's most important arguments.

- **Connect to students' media lives:** Ask students to analyze their own news consumption using Rosen's concepts. Where do they encounter "communication without community"? How is their attention being "gained"?

### Known Challenges

- **Dense theoretical sections:** Chapters 4 and 7, which engage with crowd psychology and democratic theory, can be challenging. Provide reading guides or focus questions to help students navigate.

- **Historical distance:** Students may not be familiar with references to the penny press, Johnny Carson, etc. Brief contextual introductions help.

- **The "so what" question:** Some students may wonder why structural critique matters if it doesn't immediately suggest solutions. Frame the dissertation as providing diagnostic tools—you can't fix what you can't see.

- **Rosen's ongoing work:** Students may find more recent Rosen writing that seems to contradict or modify dissertation claims. This is an opportunity to discuss intellectual evolution.
