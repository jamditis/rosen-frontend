## Introduction: Journalism as a Transaction

### Comprehension
1. How does Rosen define journalism as a "transaction"? Who are the parties involved?
2. What does Rosen mean by the "ecological view" of press and public?
3. Why does Rosen argue that "providing information is not all there is to informing the public"?

### Analysis
1. How does framing journalism as a "transaction" differ from viewing it as something journalists simply "do"?
2. What does Rosen suggest is missing from conventional press criticism focused on accuracy and bias?

### Application
1. Think of a recent news story you encountered. Using Rosen's framework, what "transaction" took place? What was the symbolic environment in which it occurred?

## Chapter 1: Democracy and Distance

### Comprehension
1. What does Rosen mean when he says "news arrives from a distance"?
2. How did the scale of American republican government create the need for newspapers?
3. Why did early newspapers provide almost no local news?

### Analysis
1. How does Rosen use the concept of "imagined communities" to explain the newspaper's role?
2. What is the difference between a press that "informs" publics and one that "forms" them?

### Application

## Chapter 3: The Universal Town Meeting

### Comprehension
1. What is the "universal town meeting" vision of the press, and why does Rosen critique it?
2. What was the Great Moon Hoax of 1835, and what does it illustrate about news and truth?
3. What does Rosen mean when he says "accuracy is a relative term"?

### Analysis
1. How can accuracy be a function of "fit with audience expectations" rather than correspondence with external reality?
2. What is the difference between "circulating information" and "forming opinion"?

### Application

## Chapter 4: From Crowd to Public

### Comprehension
1. How does Rosen distinguish between "crowd" and "public"?
2. What role did crowd psychology (LeBon, Tarde) play in understanding early newspaper audiences?
3. What is unique about the social body created by newspapers?

### Analysis
1. How can people act collectively without physically gathering together?
2. Why does Rosen engage with theories of "hypnosis" and "suggestion" when discussing media influence?

### Application
1. Are social media users a "crowd" or a "public" in Rosen's terms? Consider phenomena like viral moments, Twitter mobs, and online movements.

## Chapter 5: Communication Without Community

### Comprehension
1. What does Rosen mean by "communication without community"?
2. What is the "space of collected attention"?
3. What does the phrase "public transmission, private reception" describe?

### Analysis
1. How does entertainment function as "the search for symbolic forms that bring members of an audience together on no other, more solid basis than common consumption"?
2. Why does Rosen argue that "sensationalism is what happens when communication outruns the sense of community"?

### Application
1. Does contemporary digital communication represent "communication without community"? Give specific examples from platforms like TikTok, Instagram, or YouTube.
2. Are there examples of digital media creating genuine community rather than just collected attention?

## Chapter 6: The Impossible Press

### Comprehension
1. What makes the press "impossible" according to Rosen?
2. What is the difference between "attention granted" and "attention gained"?
3. What is the "golden age" of press criticism, and what did critics recognize during this period?

### Analysis
1. How did professionalism promise to resolve the conflict between the newspaper's commercial basis and public functions?
2. Why does Rosen argue that professionalism framed a "structural problem as a matter of individual conduct"?
3. How does this chapter serve as the culmination of the dissertation's argument?

### Application
1. Is the press more or less "impossible" today than in 1986? Consider the rise of digital advertising, attention metrics, and platform dependencies.
2. How do contemporary journalists navigate the tension between gaining attention and serving the public interest?
