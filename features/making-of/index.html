The making of

Jay Rosen's Internet Archive

Jay Rosen spent four decades publishing across dozens of sites, and the web was quietly deleting the record as it went. I spent about a year turning what survived into one searchable archive, built so it keeps working after the tools that made it are gone. This is how that actually went, mistakes included.

The story runs two ways. The case file is the evidence: dates, counts, receipts, and a few exhibits you can check. The article is the testimony: what I was thinking, and where I was wrong. Switch anytime.

By Joe Amditis. Source of record: the archive's own docs/narrative and the project's public git history.

CH.01  /  origin

The itch

Where this really starts

I have been trying to scrape things off the internet since about 2014. My first GitHub account, long gone now, was full of half-working experiments for pulling pages down and pinning them in place before they vanished. So when the chance came to build an archive of Jay Rosen's work, it landed on an itch I had been scratching for a decade.

Jay has taught journalism at NYU since 1986, and he wrote in public the whole time: the PressThink blog, op-eds, academic papers, panels on YouTube, thousands of tweets, Bluesky threads, Tumblr posts from his Studio 20 years. That is the problem in one sentence. The work is everywhere, and everywhere is not a place you can keep. Publications merge, fold, and get bought by people who do not care. Links rot. You can be an intellectual in public for forty years and watch the record of it thin out behind you.

The brief was easy to say and hard to do: put everything of Jay's that has a URL in one place, and make it searchable. Not a list of links. A list of links rots at the same rate as the web. I wanted a structured record: each piece with its text, its date, its ideas, and its connections to his other ideas, so that "the view from nowhere" or "the people formerly known as the audience" is something you can follow through the work, not just a phrase you can find.

And I wanted it to outlast me. That is the part I kept circling. I meant survive the contract, the tools, the platforms, and eventually the web it was scraped from.

You can be an intellectual in public for forty years and watch the record of it thin out behind you.

CH.02  /  2025, learning the tools

Learning git in public

The unflattering early chapters

I have to tell you how bad I was at this at the start, because the archive's own history is written into the thing it lives in, and the early chapters are not flattering.

I did not really understand git when I began. I knew it the way you know a city from a map: I had the names, not the streets. My first real repository for the project has 111 commits on a single branch, and ten of them are titled "Add files via upload." That is the message GitHub writes for you when you drag files into the browser instead of pushing them from your machine. I was not using version control. I was using a website as a hard drive with a nicer icon.

It got more expensive than embarrassing. Early on I committed an API key where it did not belong and woke up to an email telling me it had been running up a bill overnight before I caught it. Another time a scraper of mine tripped a rate limit so hard that GitHub disabled every automated action on my account for looking like abuse. And at one point I deleted the whole repository and started over, and lost about a month of the project's history doing it. The archive is supposed to be a record that survives. I erased a chunk of its own record by hand.

What fixed it was not a tutorial. It was the second and third repositories. The second one had real dependency management and pull requests, but I also committed the dissertation's PDFs straight into it, which is what you are specifically not supposed to do, and crammed five half-built frontends in side by side. The third one, the one still standing, finally did the boring correct thing: one job per repository, secrets kept out, changes made on a branch and reviewed before they land. I learned separation of concerns the way most people do, which is by not doing it first.

I was using a website as a hard drive with a nicer icon.

CH.03  /  2025, the pipeline

The pipeline and its potholes

June to October 2025

The machine that fills the archive is a Python pipeline: take a URL, fetch the page, pull out the text, ask a model to read it, write the result to a row. Simple to describe. The first bugs were the stupid, expensive kind. A missing audio module turned out to be Python 3.12 dropping something the old code leaned on, so I pinned everything back to 3.11. An "API key not valid" error that cost me an afternoon was curly quotes in a config file where straight quotes belonged. The key was fine. The typography was not.

The recurring fight was scraping. The big news sites do not want a bot reading them, and they will block a headless browser without blinking. So the fetch became a cascade: try Google's URL Context tool first, fall back to a plain fast fetch, fall back again to a full stealth browser only when the cheaper paths failed. Cheapest tool that works, every time, because I was paying for the expensive ones.

The old PressThink archive was its own kind of hard. Years of posts lived on single enormous pages, and two different URL structures pointed at overlapping content. I had to write parsers for the giant pages and then dedupe two schemes against each other. I am still not certain I caught every collision. I expect bug reports to find the ones I missed, so I built the thing so that fixing one is a fifteen-minute job rather than a rebuild.

Scale broke things that worked fine on ten records. After the first full run the publication dates came back empty, all of them, because the sites did not put the date where the scraper looked. One backfill pass recovered 680 dates from patterns buried in the URLs, a second pulled 144 more out of page metadata, and the dataset landed at ninety-seven percent dated. Most of the pipeline's hard problems were that shape: reality arriving in a format nobody had planned for.

The worst bug of the whole project did not crash anything, which is exactly why it was the worst. A re-analysis script was catching the model's answer in a variable and writing none of it to the sheet, falling back to the same hardcoded defaults every time, so all 725 records came out reading identical. I only found it because someone asked whether the re-analysis was actually doing anything with its results. It wasn't. The same silent-write pattern surfaced a month later in a different script, that time with a price tag: about fifty cents billed across eighty rows for analysis that never got saved. The money was trivial. The lesson was the expensive part.

One decision from this stretch outlived every other one: keep the whole thing boring. No database server to run, no subscription to lapse. The data would be flat files a person could open, and the site would read them directly. That choice is the one I would defend hardest.

  • 2025-06

    Pipeline stood up. Fetch a URL, run analysis, write a row.

  • ENV

    Python 3.12 dropped audioop (pin to 3.11). Curly quotes in .env misread as a bad API key.

  • SCRAPE

    Cascade. Stage 1 URL Context, stage 2 requests + trafilatura, stage 3 Playwright + stealth. Cheapest path that works. Big sites threw net::ERR_HTTP2_PROTOCOL_ERROR and timeouts at the headless browser.

  • PRESSTHINK

    Years of posts on single giant pages. Two overlapping URL schemes parsed and deduped. Some collisions may remain. Fixes turn around in ~15 min.

  • DATES

    First full run: publication dates 100% empty. Backfill recovered 680 from URL patterns, then 144 from page metadata. 97% coverage.

  • INCIDENT

    2025-09. A re-analysis caught its output in a variable and never wrote it to the sheet, falling back to hardcoded defaults. 725 records held identical values. Uniqueness ratio 0.1% (expected 60–80%). Found only when someone asked if it was doing anything.

  • INCIDENT

    2025-10. Same silent-write pattern in a second script. Rows 1–80 analyzed and billed (~$0.53) with zero fields written. Fixed with per-field write verification.

  • DECISION

    Zero server, zero subscription. Flat files the site reads directly. The durability spine.

CH.04  /  2025–2026, the machines

Working with the machines

The part people assume built the whole thing

People assume this archive was built by AI, and in a narrow sense it was. A model reads every record and proposes its summary, its themes, and the entities it names. I have leaned on Gemini for that since the first version, called through an API and not a chat window, because I needed it in a script, running the same way ten thousand times. Later I added review models to the workflow: Codex reading my code before a change lands, a second model as a tie-breaker. The building of this thing is soaked in AI.

The finished thing is not. The building of it uses AI to make something that does not need AI to keep working. Open the archive and there is no model in the loop. It is flat files and a small amount of code reading them in your browser. If every AI company folded tomorrow, the archive would not notice. That was a design goal, not an accident. Models get expensive, get worse, get discontinued, or decide you are no longer welcome.

The machines also taught me not to trust them, and that lesson has a price tag. The silent failures from the last chapter, the 725 identical records and the sibling bug that billed for work it never saved, became a rule I have not broken since: a script that does not crash is not a script that worked. Verify the output in the actual data store. Test on five rows, then twenty-five, then a hundred, never straight to the full set. If it cost money, confirm the money bought something. I also let the plain tool win when it was better. For reading text off scanned newspaper clippings I tried the impressive options and shipped Tesseract, an open-source OCR engine older than most of the models, because it did the job and it will still be here in ten years.

The version history has the receipt for that last choice. There is a commit in the first repo whose entire message is "Update OCR system name from DeepSeek to Tesseract." That is the moment the flashy option lost to the durable one.

The building of it uses AI to make something that does not need AI to keep working.

  • ANALYSIS

    Gemini via API from v0.0.1, in a script, not a chat window. Produces summaries, themes, and entities per record.

  • 2025-08

    Google URL Context tool becomes stage 1 of the scrape cascade.

  • REVIEW

    Codex reads code before merge (from 2025-10). CI plus a second review model added 2025-12. Pre-PR codex review and review subagents by 2026.

  • OCR

    Tesseract (open-source) chosen over model-based OCR for scanned clippings. Recorded in git as a rename commit.

  • TEST

    A 42-row production test scored articles 25/25 and multimedia 10/16, turning up six edge-case classes the paper design had missed.

  • INVARIANT

    No model in the runtime. Flat files read in-browser. The archive functions with every AI vendor gone.

CH.05  /  2025, the graph

From records to a web of ideas

October to December 2025

A pile of records is not an archive of ideas. By the fall of 2025 I had a few hundred pieces, each sitting alone, and no way to ask the question the archive existed to answer: how do Jay's ideas connect. Entity extraction is what turned the pile into a web. A model reads each record and pulls out the people, organizations, and concepts in it. Then the hard part starts, which is deciding that "The New York Times," "NY Times," and "NYT" are one thing and not three. That deduplication layer, resolving every name to a single canonical entity, is the piece that made the graph mean anything.

The first full run took about eight hours. The current graph holds 8,152 entities and 12,560 relationships. Read back, it puts Jay at the center by a wide margin, with the Times, PressThink, and the Washington Post as his nearest neighbors, which is roughly what four decades of media criticism should look like.

It also handed me a clean lesson in believing your own output. An early analysis produced a tidy claim that Jay wrote about building journalism four times as often as criticizing it. It was wrong: the extractor had quietly merged "founded" and "pioneered" into one bucket, and the false ratio had spread into more than twenty files before I caught it.

  • 2025-10

    A few hundred records, flat. No relationship queries possible.

  • METHOD

    Model extracts entities. An in-memory registry dedupes names to canonical IDs (P-00001 Jay Rosen, O-00001 The New York Times).

  • RUN 1

    ~8 hrs. Schema revised to stop self-referential entities ("Jay Rosen founded [this post]").

  • SCHEMA

    Relationship vocabulary grew 10 to 15 types after a 100-record run turned up ownership, founding, and influence links the schema had been rejecting.

  • AUGMENT

    An early augmentation run wrote malformed ids (REL-06161, UNKNOWN sources, blank entities). Those rows were deleted and rerun clean before the batch was trusted.

  • GRAPH

    Current: 8,152 entities, 12,560 relationships. Jay is the central node. Nearest: NYT, PressThink, Washington Post.

  • LESSON

    A "4:1 build-vs-criticize" claim was a founded/pioneered conflation. Reached 20+ files before removal.

CH.06  /  2025, the design

Making it look like what it is

December 2025, version 2.26.0

For most of 2025 the archive looked like every other web app: bright blue, bright pink, white background, the default look of a thing built fast. It worked and it was wrong. It did not look like an archive of journalism history. It looked like a dashboard.

The fix was to tie every visual choice to a physical thing. The background became aged newsprint. The cards became cream card stock. The bright blue became the faded ink of an old front page, the green a card-catalog green, the accent a manila-folder tan. Paper grain, dog-eared corners, filing-tab notches. The page you are reading now is built from that same palette, on purpose: the archive and its own making-of should feel like they came out of the same drawer.

There was a process lesson here too, and it was about restraint. My first plan was to send a swarm of automated edits through the components in five passes. They mixed styles, broke layouts, and I reverted all of it. What worked was narrow: let the machines inventory and scaffold, and keep the taste, the deciding, in human hands, tested on one page before it touched the app.

It worked and it was wrong.

  • 2025-12

    Frontend reads generic. Bright Tailwind (sky-500, pink-500), white ground. Verdict: not an archive.

  • v2.26.0

    Vintage archive aesthetic. Each color mapped to a material.

  • COLOR

    Newsprint #f5f1e8, cream stock #fdfcf9, faded ink #2c5f82, catalog green #3a5f3f, manila tan #d4a574.

  • INFRA

    ~200 CSS tokens, reusable components, still zero build.

  • PROCESS

    Five-wave automated edits broke layouts, reverted. Machines scaffold, humans decide, test on a demo page first.

CH.07  /  2026, the handoff

The part nobody automates

The last hundred feet

The archive was built and the automatic publish was built, and getting the finished files onto the live site turned into its own small project: the way the site had been set up years earlier took real work to untangle before anything could go out the door. The link rot the archive fights had reached the archive's own front door.

Once a week I email Jay a zip file, he opens it, and he drags the new files into a web uploader by hand. That is the deploy pipeline right now. It is clunky and it works.

The data is not finished either. It is around ninety-five or ninety-six percent of the way there, with a soft spot roughly from 2004 to 2008 where the record thins out: publications that folded, or that never archived themselves. Looking at that stretch with Jay once, I told him I thought he had written more than we had been able to find. I still think so, and I have not found it.

The link rot the archive fights had reached the archive's own front door.

  • DEPLOY

    Automatic publish built, not yet wired up. Updated by hand in the meantime.

  • SETUP

    The original hosting setup predated the archive and took time to untangle before the first publish.

  • FALLBACK

    Weekly, the curator receives a zip and uploads files by hand through a web file manager. Clunky, working.

  • QUALITY

    ~95–96%. Soft coverage roughly 2004–2008 (folded or unarchived publications).

  • CAPACITY

    Solo and unpaid. New records added by hand. No standing technical maintainer on the archive's side.

CH.08  /  ongoing

A record of the record

The test that has not happened yet

The launch did not finish the archive. Once other people could see the structure, the errors in it were easy to find: counts in the docs that no longer matched the data, records with empty text, threads with placeholder titles, a claim in a file that the system had outgrown. So the archive started keeping a record of itself, and checking that record against reality.

That is the rule the whole thing runs on now. Every change goes through a branch and a review before it lands. Nothing merges itself. When a document says the system does something, that sentence gets checked against the system, because I have been burned by a confident wrong sentence before. This page is part of that same habit. I wrote it from the project's own history and its git log, and I checked every number on it against the live files the day it went up. Some of those numbers are gaps, and I left them on the page instead of rounding them off: seventy-nine records still unverified, a handful of them genuinely gone, a print-only piece from 1999, a monograph from a civic-journalism center that no longer exists. An archive that only reports its wins is not keeping an honest record.

The real test of this thing is not today. It is a day years from now, when the model that reads these records is discontinued, or the company that made it is gone, or the contract was never renewed and nobody is being paid to care. On that day, someone types a name into the archive and it answers, out of flat files, in a browser, with no machine in the loop. If that happens, it worked.

An archive that only reports its wins is not keeping an honest record.

  • 2025-12

    Errors found after launch: stale counts, empty text, placeholder titles, doc claims out of sync.

  • DISCIPLINE

    Branch and review on every change. No self-merge. Docs checked against the system.

  • OPEN

    79 records unverified (2026-07-07). Some unrecoverable: a 1999 print-only piece, a defunct civic-journalism center's monograph.

  • OPEN

    Coverage soft spot roughly 2004–2008.

  • TEST

Status: open

Three rules the archive won't break

  • Raw text is preserved. Where a record has raw text, any processing must keep it intact. A change that destroys raw_text on an existing row is treated as a defect.
  • CSVs are parsed with a real parser. The data holds quoted fields with embedded commas and newlines, so it is read with Python's csv module, never with grep, cut, or awk.
  • Dissertation quotes are verified. Every quote and attribution from the 1986 dissertation was checked against the original text, and must not be modified, paraphrased, or fabricated.
