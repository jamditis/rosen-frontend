# The art and science of forming a public

## What the chapter argues

In this chapter Rosen turns from Lippmann's diagnosis to Dewey's response. Dewey accepted that the classical informed citizen could not survive modern conditions, but he rejected the conclusion that experts should govern in the public's place. His answer was to rebuild community as the ground on which a public could form. There is no public sitting in wait for the morning's news. A public is something that has to be made, assembled around a shared understanding of common problems. And the communication that forms it is an art rather than a science: like a farmer sowing seeds, the communicator prepares the ground, plants with care, and lets understanding take root and grow over time.

## From The Impossible Press

Seeds are sown not by being thrown out at random, but by being so distributed as to take root and have a chance of growth. (Chapter 8, p. 330)
