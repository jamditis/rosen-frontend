# Democracy and distance

## What the chapter argues

Rosen opens The Impossible Press by tracing the need for journalism to a single condition: distance. News only becomes necessary when events unfold beyond the reach of direct experience, so the press is what crosses the space between people and the events they cannot witness. He reads the Romans as the first civilization to systematically need news, because their empire stretched beyond what word of mouth could carry. The American republic created a public on a still larger scale, one whose geographic spread made newspaper communication essential to democratic participation. The chapter sets up the tension that runs through the rest of the dissertation: democratic theory was developed for small, self-contained communities, and extending it across a continent asks the press to do work that face-to-face contact once did.

## The figures in this chapter

Jefferson appears as the thinker who understood the problem of scale most clearly. He advocated ward republics, dividing counties into small units where citizens could meet and deliberate directly, precisely because he believed genuine democracy required limits on size. When that direct contact is impossible, the newspaper steps in as a substitute, raising the question of whether mediated communication can truly replace face-to-face deliberation. The chapter also introduces the idea that newspaper readers form an imagined community, bound together only by reading the same information, never by meeting.
