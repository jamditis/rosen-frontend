# The myth of the omnicompetent citizen

## What the chapter argues

In the second part of the dissertation, Rosen turns to the professionalized press and the critiques of it offered by Walter Lippmann and John Dewey. This chapter draws on Lippmann to make a hard claim: the democratic ideal of a citizen who can hold informed opinions on every public question is, in Lippmann's word, entirely unworkable. The reason is not laziness or bad journalism. People respond not to reality but to representations of reality, the pictures in their heads, and those pictures are built from stereotypes and prior belief as much as from fact. Rosen uses this to separate two things the older theory treated as one: releasing facts into the world, and informing the public. The chapter insists the second does not follow from the first.

## Why it matters

If the trouble lies deeper than the press, then so does the remedy. Rosen reads Lippmann as exposing the limits of any press reform that only asks journalists to publish more or publish better. The pseudo-environment, the stereotype, and the limits of attention and complexity are structural facts about how people take in the world, not faults to be corrected by harder work at the newspaper. The chapter sets up the rest of Part Two: if the informed citizen is impossible on the old terms, the question becomes what a press could realistically do, and what would have to change beyond it.

## From The Impossible Press

One could free all the facts in the world and still not be informing the public, for the important question was not what was released into the environment, but what took shape in the beliefs and behavior of the average citizen. (Chapter 7, p. 280)
