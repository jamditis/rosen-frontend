# John Dewey

## Who he is

John Dewey was a philosopher and educational reformer. Rosen cites him for his response to Walter Lippmann's argument about the public: Dewey accepted Lippmann's diagnosis that the citizen could not be the informed actor democratic theory assumed, but rejected his elitist remedy. Dewey argued instead that a public is not found but formed, created through shared understanding of common problems, and that the cure for public life lay in rebuilding community.

## In the dissertation

In Part Two, Rosen sets Dewey against Lippmann as he traces how journalism professionalized after 1900 to reconcile the business of news with its claim to public service (p. 198). In Chapter 8 he develops Dewey's position at length: Dewey agreed with Lippmann's diagnosis but called for the reconstruction of community as the basis for a revitalized public, holding that there is no public out there waiting to be informed (p. 330). Rosen draws on Dewey's claim that a public must be actively created (p. 345) and on his idea of converting the Great Society into a Great Community (p. 340). Dewey also gives Rosen the figure of communication as an art: seeds are sown not by being thrown out at random, but by being so distributed as to take root and have a chance of growth.
