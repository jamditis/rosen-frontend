# Walter Lippmann

## Who he is

The archive records Walter Lippmann as a writer, reporter, and political commentator. In The Impossible Press, Rosen treats him as the first to recognize that the classical democratic ideal of the informed citizen was incompatible with modern conditions. Lippmann argued that people respond not to the world itself but to representations of it, the "pictures in their heads," and that the stereotype is a form of perception that works from private belief toward reality: we define first and then see. On these grounds he called the expectation that average citizens can hold informed opinions on all public questions "entirely unworkable."

## In the dissertation

Chapter 7 builds on Lippmann's diagnosis of the pseudo-environment: people act on "pictures in their heads" rather than on reality, so releasing facts into the world is not the same as informing the public (p. 268, p. 280). The omnicompetence myth, the assumption that citizens can be informed on every public question, is the expectation Lippmann judged unworkable (p. 310). In Part Two and Chapter 8, Rosen pairs Lippmann with John Dewey, who agreed with the diagnosis but rejected the elitist solution and argued for rebuilding community as the basis for a revitalized public (p. 198, p. 330).

## From The Impossible Press

"One could free all the facts in the world and still not be informing the public, for the important question was not what was released into the environment, but what took shape in the beliefs and behavior of the average citizen." (Chapter 7, p. 268)
