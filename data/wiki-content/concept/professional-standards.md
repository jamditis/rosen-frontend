# Professional standards

## The idea

After yellow journalism, the press needed a way to show that it took its public duties seriously. The answer was a set of professional standards: objectivity, accuracy, fairness, and completeness. These became the marks of responsible journalism, the qualities a reporter could point to as proof of good faith. For Rosen, this turn to standards is the heart of what he calls the impossible press, because it tried to settle the whole problem of public life by controlling the conduct of journalists. The trouble is that standards govern how a journalist behaves; they do not, by themselves, reach the deeper question of whether the press can inform a public at all.

## Objectivity as a method for managing controversy

Objectivity was the standard that promised the most. It offered neutrality and truth, a way of reporting the world without the reporter's own slant. Rosen reads it more skeptically. Objectivity worked as a method for managing controversy rather than as a way of achieving understanding. It gave journalists a defensible procedure for handling disputed claims, but procedure is not the same as comprehension, and a press organized around staying out of the fight is not necessarily a press that helps a public understand what is at stake.

## The limits of standards

Professional standards run into a wall that no amount of care can climb. They assume the public exists and is ready to be informed. They cannot create a public where none exists. A journalist can be accurate, fair, and complete and still be writing into a void, because accuracy and fairness are answers to the question of how to report, not to the prior question of whether there is a formed public capable of acting on the report. This is why Rosen treats the standards as assuming too much: they take the existence of their audience for granted and so leave the hardest part of the work undone.

## From The Impossible Press

An impossible press was born, one which sought to solve the whole problem of public life simply by controlling the conduct of journalists. (Chapter 6, p. 220)
