# The yellow journalism crisis

## The idea

Yellow journalism was the sensational press of Joseph Pulitzer and William Randolph Hearst. Its excesses created a crisis of credibility that did more than embarrass the trade: it drove the professionalization movement. By putting a sharper edge on the conflict between private profits and public duties, yellow journalism made the gap between journalism as a business and journalism as a public service impossible to ignore, and the professional reforms that followed were an answer to that crisis.

## The Spanish-American War

The clearest cautionary tale was the Spanish-American War. Yellow journalism's role in promoting the war became the standard example of what an irresponsible press could do, a case study in press power and the duty that is supposed to come with it. The episode showed that a sensational press did not merely entertain; it could help push a country toward war, and that possibility hung over every later argument about the responsibilities of journalism.

## The professional reaction

The crisis produced a reaction aimed at restoring credibility. It led to journalism schools, professional associations, and codes of ethics, the institutional machinery of a profession. These were attempts to repair the damage by changing how journalists were trained and held to account. In Rosen's reading this reaction is the origin of the impossible press: the hope that better-trained, better-governed journalists could resolve a conflict that runs deeper than the conduct of any individual reporter.
