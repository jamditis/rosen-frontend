# Jefferson's dilemma

## The idea

Jefferson grasped a problem at the heart of large-scale democracy: real deliberation happens when citizens can meet, talk, and decide together, and that is only possible in a small, self-contained community. His advocacy for ward republics was not a side project but a direct response to this limit. He understood that an informed public, in the full sense, depends on a scale where people can experience public matters firsthand rather than learn about them at a distance. Rosen treats this as a dilemma because the republic Jefferson helped found was already too large for the ward to be the unit of its public life.

## Ward republics

The ward republic was Jefferson's concrete proposal for keeping democracy small enough to work. He suggested dividing counties into small wards where citizens could meet face-to-face and deliberate on public matters directly, rather than delegating that work upward to representatives or relying on accounts of events they never saw. The ward was an attempt to preserve, inside a continental republic, the conditions under which a public could actually form and act.

## The press as substitute

Where the ward could not reach, the newspaper took its place. The press became a substitute for the direct communication possible in small communities, carrying news of public matters to people who could no longer gather to discuss them. The dilemma is whether that substitute is adequate: can a newspaper truly replace face-to-face deliberation, or does mediated communication change the nature of the public life it claims to sustain? Rosen leaves the question open here, but it sets up the larger argument that communication is not the same as community.
