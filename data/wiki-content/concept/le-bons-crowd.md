# Le Bon's crowd

## The idea

Gustave Le Bon held that a person inside a crowd is not the same person as that individual alone. In the crowd, Rosen recounts, the individual loses the rational faculties that ordinarily govern judgment and becomes open to suggestion and to the spread of emotion. The crowd thinks and acts by contagion rather than by deliberation, which is why it seemed to nineteenth century observers both powerful and dangerous. Le Bon's crowd supplies the darker half of the picture against which the idea of a public is later set: if the mass of newspaper readers were simply a crowd at scale, then the press would be a machine for spreading suggestion rather than informing citizens.

## Place in the chapter

Rosen draws on Le Bon to mark the anxiety that the mass press inherited. The worry was not only that crowds gather in the street but that the newspaper might assemble a crowd at a distance, an audience susceptible to the same loss of reason and the same emotional contagion without ever being in one place. Tarde's account of the public is the answer the chapter offers to that worry, distinguishing the dispersed reading public from the irrational physical crowd.
