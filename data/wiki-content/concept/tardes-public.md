# Tarde's public

## The idea

Gabriel Tarde drew the line that gives the chapter its title. Where Le Bon's crowd is a body of people pressed together in one place, Tarde's public is dispersed. Its members are scattered and may never meet, yet they form a single social body because they attend to the same media at the same time. The bond is shared attention rather than physical proximity. This is the figure that lets Rosen describe the readers of the mass newspaper as a public: connected not by being in a crowd but by reading the same paper, fixed on the same events from a distance.

## Place in the chapter

Tarde's distinction is the hinge of the move from crowd to public. It reframes the nineteenth century fear of irrational masses by proposing a different kind of aggregation, one made possible by the newspaper itself. The dispersed public is calmer than the crowd, but, as the chapter goes on to argue, the attention that holds it together is shared and abstract rather than rooted in face-to-face life, which is what makes the resulting bonds unstable and the public liable to scatter when attention shifts.
